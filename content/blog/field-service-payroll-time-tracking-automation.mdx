**Manual time tracking costs the average field service business 1–8% of its annual payroll in errors alone.** On a $400,000 payroll — typical for a 4-tech HVAC or plumbing shop — that is $4,000 to $32,000 per year vanishing into wrong hours, missed overtime, and admin corrections. And that number only counts what you can see. It does not count the **15–20% of billable hours** that field service businesses lose each year simply because nobody captured the time accurately enough to invoice it.

The setup is familiar: techs fill out paper timesheets at the end of the day, or the end of the week, or whenever someone reminds them. The office reconciles those sheets against the job list, finds the discrepancies, and spends hours chasing down who worked what on which job. Payroll goes out — probably right, probably not — and the job costing numbers that come out the other side are too distorted to actually manage the business with.

This is not a people problem. It is a system problem. And it has a system solution.

## What Manual Time Tracking Is Actually Costing You

Before getting to the fix, it is worth doing the math for your operation specifically.

A field service company with five technicians, each billing an average of $85/hour and working 40 hours per week, should capture roughly **$850,000 in annual billable labor**. If poor time tracking causes 15% of those hours to go unrecorded or under-billed — a conservative estimate based on industry patterns — that is **$127,500 per year in unbilled labor**. The work was done. The technician was on-site. The business just never collected for it.

Then there is the payroll side. Studies cited by payroll processors put **approximately 20% of payroll records containing at least one error**, with each error costing an estimated **$291 to identify and correct** (a figure attributed to EY, though noted as an industry estimate). For a business running bi-weekly payroll with 10 employees, that is roughly $400–$500 per pay cycle in administrative rework — over **$10,000 per year** just in error correction overhead.

And then there is the time cost. Processing payroll manually — collecting timesheets, reconciling hours, handling exceptions, running payroll — takes **5 to 10 hours per pay cycle** for a team of five or more, or roughly **120 to 240 hours per year** for the person running it. At $30/hour of admin or owner time, that is $3,600 to $7,200 per year spent solely on the mechanics of paying people.

Add these up and the total annual drag of manual time tracking in a mid-sized service business is real money — often more than the annual cost of the software that eliminates it.

## Three Places Time Tracking Breaks in Field Service

The specific failure points are predictable. They show up the same way across HVAC companies, plumbing shops, landscaping crews, and restoration businesses.

**The travel time gap.** A technician leaves the shop at 7:30 AM and arrives on-site at 8:15 AM. The job sheet says the job started at 8:00 AM — or 7:30 AM — depending on how the tech filled it in and what they remembered. Travel time between jobs, return trips to the shop for parts, and time spent on dispatch calls often disappears entirely or gets assigned to the wrong job. At scale, this distorts both billing and job costing.

**Multi-job days with one timesheet.** A tech completes a service call in the morning and a repair in the afternoon. At end of day, they fill in one timesheet from memory: seven hours total, two jobs. How much time went to each? The honest answer is that nobody knows exactly. The numbers they write down are approximations. When those approximations feed job costing, the job costing becomes a fiction.

**The end-of-week reconstruction.** For crews that submit timesheets weekly rather than daily, the problem compounds. Friday afternoon, a tech fills in five days of work from memory. They round hours to the nearest half-day. They forget the Thursday callback. They omit the Wednesday drive to the supply house. That reconstruction is what your payroll is based on.

## The Automation Stack That Fixes It

The fix is not asking techs to be more careful. It is removing the act of remembering from the process entirely.

Here is what a fully automated time tracking and payroll workflow looks like in practice, step by step.

**Step 1 — GPS-triggered clock-in at job site arrival.**
The technician's phone detects arrival at a job address within a defined geofence (typically 100–300 feet). The time tracking app — ClockShark, Homebase, or the native time tracking module in Jobber or ServiceTitan — automatically logs the clock-in and attaches it to the specific job in the schedule. The tech does not press a button. Time starts when they arrive because the system knows when they arrived.

This eliminates the most common source of error: the difference between when a tech actually started and what they wrote down later.

**Step 2 — Job-linked time tracking through the field service app.**
Every time entry is attached to a job record, not just a date. When the tech is at Job A, their time flows to Job A. When they drive to the shop for parts, that time is logged as drive/non-billable. When they arrive at Job B, the new clock-in starts. This granularity does two things: it gives you accurate billing data and accurate job costing data — which are only the same thing if you are tracking at the job level.

**Step 3 — Tech marks job complete, time record locks.**
When the technician marks a job complete in the field service app, the time entry locks to that job and is no longer editable from the mobile device without manager override. This prevents backdating and after-the-fact adjustments that are the primary mechanism of timecard errors. The job record now has a verified start time, end time, and total labor hours attached.

**Step 4 — Daily exception notifications to the manager.**
Instead of discovering problems at payroll time, the system sends a daily summary: who clocked in and out on what jobs, any clock-ins more than 15 minutes outside the scheduled window, and any jobs that closed without a corresponding clock-out. The manager reviews a one-screen summary each morning rather than a week of paper timesheets each Friday. Issues get caught while the information is still fresh — not two weeks after the job.

**Step 5 — Overtime alert when a tech approaches 40 hours.**
When any technician approaches 35 hours in the week, the system fires an alert to the dispatcher and manager. For businesses operating in California or other states with daily overtime rules, the alert triggers at 8 hours in a day. This eliminates the most expensive payroll surprise: discovering on Friday that two techs have already hit 42 hours and you have not yet moved the Thursday service calls.

**Step 6 — Automatic payroll sync at the end of the pay period.**
Approved time records push automatically to the payroll platform — Gusto, QuickBooks Payroll, ADP Run, or similar — without any manual export, re-entry, or spreadsheet work. The hours are already sorted by employee, already tagged with job codes, already flagged for overtime. Running payroll becomes a review-and-approve process, not a data-entry process.

This six-step workflow does not require any change in how your technicians do their jobs. It requires that they have the app installed on their phones and that your jobs are scheduled in a system that the time tracking integrates with — which is true for any business using Jobber, ServiceTitan, Housecall Pro, Workiz, or any comparable field service platform.

## How Job Costing Becomes Useful Again

The downstream benefit that takes most businesses by surprise is what accurate time data does to job costing.

Right now, if you were to look at your most profitable job type versus your least profitable, the variance is probably less meaningful than it appears — because the labor hours attached to each are approximations. The $1,200 drain replacement that looked like an 85% margin job might actually be a 60% margin job once you count the two additional hours of troubleshooting that the tech forgot to log.

When time flows automatically to job records, you get a real picture. After 90 days on a fully automated system, most service businesses can see clearly which job types run over their estimated hours, which technicians complete jobs faster than the schedule assumes, and which service areas involve enough travel time to erode profitability. That data is what changes pricing, dispatch decisions, and hiring plans — and it only exists if the time tracking is accurate.

## What to Track Once It's Live

Five numbers tell you whether the system is working.

1. **Payroll error rate** — how many corrections or adjustments does your payroll require each cycle? Pre-automation, most field service businesses average two to four corrections per cycle. Target: **zero adjustments** after the first 30 days of clean data flow.

2. **Payroll processing time** — total hours from "pay period closes" to "payroll submitted." Pre-automation baseline: 5–10 hours. Post-automation target: **under 90 minutes**, covering review and approval only.

3. **Estimated vs. actual hours by job type** — after 60 days on accurate tracking, compare your scheduled job hours against actual logged hours for each service type. Any job type where actual consistently exceeds estimated by 20% or more is either underpriced or the schedule assumptions are wrong. This number is not visible at all without job-level time tracking.

4. **Overtime rate by technician** — what percentage of each tech's total hours are overtime? If the top earner in overtime is always the same technician, the scheduling system is not distributing load evenly. If overtime spikes on the same days every week, the dispatch pattern is predictable and fixable.

5. **Billable vs. non-billable time ratio** — what percentage of each technician's logged hours are attached to billable jobs versus drive time, shop time, training, or callbacks? A ratio below 70% billable on a 40-hour week means your effective billing rate per employee is significantly lower than your rate card suggests. This is the metric most businesses have no visibility into — until they track time at the job level.

Run these numbers for the first 90 days after going live. The payroll processing time and error rate improve immediately. The job costing and billing metrics require 60–90 days of clean data before the patterns are reliable.

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Q: What is the most common cause of payroll errors in field service businesses?**
Manual time entry — specifically, technicians filling in timesheets from memory at the end of the day or week — is responsible for the majority of field service payroll errors. The second most common cause is the split between total hours worked and hours attached to specific jobs, which creates discrepancies between what payroll records show and what the job costing records show.

**Q: How much time does automated payroll processing save compared to manual?**
Most field service businesses running 5–15 employees spend 5–10 hours per pay cycle processing payroll manually. With automated time tracking synced directly to a payroll platform, that drops to under 90 minutes — a review and approval step only. Industry estimates put the annual savings at 100–200 hours of admin time, depending on team size.

**Q: Does GPS time tracking create privacy issues with technicians?**
Most technicians accept GPS clock-in without friction when it is framed correctly: the system tracks when they are on the clock, not 24 hours a day, and the data is used to ensure accurate pay — which protects them as much as the business. Geofencing (clock-in triggers when they arrive at the job site) is less intrusive than continuous location tracking and handles the legitimate concern that field workers raise about being monitored off the clock.

**Q: What payroll platforms integrate with field service time tracking software?**
The most common integrations: ClockShark with Gusto and QuickBooks Payroll; Homebase with Gusto, QuickBooks, and ADP Run; ServiceTitan with ADP and QuickBooks Payroll; and QuickBooks Time (TSheets) natively with QuickBooks Online. If you are running a payroll platform not on this list, most time tracking tools export a standard file format that any payroll processor can import.
