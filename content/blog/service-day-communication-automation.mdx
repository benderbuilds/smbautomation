**The average HVAC company running five trucks gets 40 to 60 inbound calls every week asking the same question: where is my technician?**

Each call takes 3 to 5 minutes to handle. Someone has to answer, pull up the schedule, locate the tech, estimate an arrival time, and relay it back to a customer who is already frustrated because they've been waiting. At 50 calls per week at four minutes each, that's **200 minutes — more than three hours — of dispatcher time every week** spent answering a question that a two-sentence automated text message would have prevented.

That's the measurable waste. The harder-to-see part is what happens when the dispatcher is tied up on a "where is my tech?" call and a new lead hits the phone. The average service business loses between **35 and 50% of calls that aren't answered within the first two minutes**. That missed call isn't an inconvenience — it's a $900 job that went to whoever picked up.

Service-day communication automation fixes the source problem. It's not a complicated build, but most businesses don't have it — and the ones that do often run on half-measures: a manual text from the tech when they remember, a vague note in the job description, a dispatcher who tries to call ahead when things aren't too hectic. None of that is a system. What follows is.

## What Customers Actually Want on Service Day

Customers who call to ask about their technician aren't demanding. They gave up half a workday, arranged coverage, or scheduled around a two-hour window. What they need is basic: **confirmation the appointment is still happening, who is coming, and when they'll arrive.**

When that information arrives proactively — before the customer starts wondering — the dynamic shifts entirely. A customer who gets a text at 8:52 AM saying "Marcus is finishing up his previous job and heading your way. Estimated arrival: 10:15–10:45 AM" doesn't call. They get a coffee, keep their morning intact, and wait for the knock.

Salesforce's 2026 service industry data puts proactive communication at the top of the list separating businesses that retain customers from those that lose them after one job. In field service specifically, businesses that send proactive updates report measurably higher satisfaction scores and lower inbound inquiry volume. The research is consistent: the call happens because customers don't have information. Give them the information, and the call doesn't happen.

## The Five-Step Service-Day Communication Sequence

Here's what a complete automated service-day communication system looks like, from dispatch assignment to job completion. Each step fires automatically — no dispatcher action required.

**Step 1 — Dispatch confirmation (tech assigned, 60–90 minutes before window):**
When a job is assigned to a technician in your dispatch software, an automated SMS fires to the customer. It includes the tech's first name, a headshot if your platform supports it, and the estimated window. Keep it concise: *"[Tech Name] is scheduled for your job today between 10 AM and 12 PM. We'll text you when they're on their way."*

This does two things. It confirms the appointment is live — customers genuinely worry about this, especially for same-day bookings. And it sets the expectation that a second message is coming, which eliminates the impulse to call.

**Step 2 — On-the-way notification (tech departs for job):**
When the tech marks themselves as "en route" in your dispatch platform, the second message fires. This one includes a real-time location tracking link — similar to how Uber shows the driver — along with the updated ETA: *"[Tech Name] is on the way! Estimated arrival in about 25 minutes. Track them here: [link]."*

This is the message that eliminates the "where is my tech?" call. It fires the moment the tech leaves — exactly when most customers start wondering if anyone is coming. Platforms like ServiceTitan, HouseCall Pro, and ServiceFusion all support this natively.

**Step 3 — Running-late alert (ETA slips by 20+ minutes):**
When a job runs long and the next customer's window gets pushed, most businesses do nothing. The customer waits through their entire window, grows frustrated, finally calls — and the dispatcher has a tense conversation instead of an easy one.

The fix: a threshold-based alert that fires automatically when the tech's running time exceeds the next job's window by more than 15 to 20 minutes. *"[Tech Name] is running longer than expected on a prior job. New estimated arrival: 12:30–1:00 PM. Sorry for the wait — we're looking forward to taking care of you."*

This message lands before the customer is angry. It acknowledges the delay. It gives a revised time. Zero dispatcher involvement required because it triggers from real-time GPS data crossing a time threshold — not from someone remembering to handle it.

**Step 4 — Arrival confirmation (tech checks in at job site):**
When the tech marks the job as started in the platform, a brief confirmation goes out: *"[Tech Name] has arrived at your location."* This is a small touch that matters disproportionately for customers who aren't home — they're at work, tracking remotely — and confirms the job actually started on schedule.

**Step 5 — Job-complete summary (tech closes out the job):**
When the job is marked complete, a summary fires automatically: what was done, any notes the tech flagged, and — if you layer this correctly — a one-tap review link. Review requests sent within **30 minutes of job completion** convert at meaningfully higher rates than those sent hours later. The customer is standing in their fixed system or freshly cooled house when the message arrives. The experience is immediate.

## The Running-Late Protocol Is the Highest-ROI Step

This warrants its own section because it's where most businesses fail — and where the ROI is highest.

A customer who waits an extra 90 minutes without communication rates the experience poorly even if the tech does excellent work. Frustration builds, impression of the company deteriorates, and when the review request arrives, the score reflects the wait, not the repair. A customer who gets a proactive delay notice at the 20-minute mark — with a revised time and a direct acknowledgment — typically doesn't penalize the company at all.

The practical difference: **businesses without running-late automation lose review stars on jobs they actually did well.** The work was fine. The communication failed. Those are different problems. Only one requires better technicians.

Here's how the threshold logic works. The dispatch platform tracks GPS position against scheduled job times. When the current job's estimated completion crosses the start window of the next job by 15–20 minutes, the system fires the delay notification automatically. The dispatcher doesn't touch it. The tech doesn't have to remember. The customer gets an updated time before they've started counting the minutes.

Some platforms call this "cascade notifications" or "smart scheduling alerts." The mechanic is the same: threshold crossed → message triggered → problem prevented.

## How Service-Day Communication Generates 5-Star Reviews

Customers don't leave five-star reviews primarily because a job was done correctly. They leave them because the experience was **different from what they expected** — specifically, more organized and more respectful of their time than they're used to.

A customer who receives a dispatch confirmation, an on-the-way text with a tracking link, a proactive delay notice when timing slips, and a job-complete summary has had an experience closer to ordering from Amazon than calling a plumber. That experience generates reviews without the business even needing to ask — though the post-job review request still helps.

The businesses with the highest Google review counts in any market aren't usually the cheapest or the longest-established. They're the ones whose customers feel informed throughout the process. Service-day communication is a significant driver of that perception.

The practical connection: if you're running a post-job review request sequence (you should be — the [automated review machine guide](/blog/automated-google-review-machine) covers that workflow in full), the response rate is substantially higher when the customer just experienced seamless service-day communication. The review request lands on top of a positive interaction, not hours later when the day has moved on.

Some businesses combine both automations in a single flow: job-complete message → 20-minute delay → review request. Short enough that the experience is still fresh, long enough that they've confirmed everything is working.

## What to Track

Four metrics tell you whether your service-day communication is working:

**1. Inbound "where is my tech?" calls per week.** Your baseline is probably 40–80 calls depending on truck count. A properly configured service-day communication sequence should cut this by **50–70% within 30 days**. Track it by having your dispatcher log inquiry-type calls by category for one week before you turn on the automation, then compare at 30 days.

**2. On-time arrival perception vs. actual on-time rate.** Your actual on-time rate (jobs completed within the promised window) might be 72%, but customers' perceived on-time rate — what they report when asked — is almost always lower. With proactive delay notifications in place, perceived on-time improves significantly even when actual performance stays flat. Track through post-job satisfaction surveys.

**3. Review request response rate.** The percentage of job-complete review requests that result in a posted review. Average response rate without a communication workflow runs 5–10%. With service-day communication in place and the review request landing in context of a smooth experience, rates of **20–30% are realistic** for businesses that combine both automations. Track in your review platform.

**4. Dispatcher handle time per shift.** Total time on inbound calls per dispatcher per day. As routine "where is my tech?" calls drop off, what remains are more complex calls — scheduling changes, complaints, estimate questions. Total call volume drops; handle time per remaining call may tick up slightly. Both are the right direction.
